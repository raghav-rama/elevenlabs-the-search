## 43. Assistant

**A memorable ad leaves someone with a simple story, a feeling, and a clear association with the brand.** You can engineer the conditions for that, then test whether it actually happens.

Start with the constraints: people give you limited attention, can follow only so much at once, and will forget most details. Your job is to decide **which tiny piece should survive**.

For AFTERHUMAN, I’d want that piece to be:

> “The implant company that lets a woman outrun the train she missed.”

That sentence contains the brand’s category, the benefit, and an image someone can retell.

Here’s how I’d build toward it:

| Feature | Why it helps | How to engineer it |
|---|---|---|
| **An immediate reason to watch** | The viewer needs something worth resolving. | Open with a problem, an unusual action, or a contradiction. Make the situation understandable immediately. |
| **A familiar human desire** | Viewers can connect unfamiliar technology to something they already care about. | Choose one desire: control, belonging, status, freedom, relief. Give it a concrete situation. |
| **One clear promise** | Multiple competing messages make the takeaway harder to retain. | Write the ad’s meaning in one sentence. Make every scene support it. |
| **Cause and effect** | Connected events give the viewer a story they can reconstruct. | Someone wants something → encounters an obstacle → uses the product → gets a changed outcome. |
| **Surprise that makes sense** | A satisfying deviation gives the story a distinctive moment. | Establish an expectation, then resolve it unexpectedly through the product’s benefit. |
| **Emotional movement** | A change in feeling gives the experience shape. | Move from frustration to anticipation to exhilaration to relief or laughter. |
| **Recognizable brand cues** | Remembering the spectacle alone doesn’t identify the advertiser. | Tie a recurring sound, shape, phrase, or character to the product and its payoff. |
| **A repeatable core** | Consistent cues across exposures give people more chances to recognize the brand. | Keep the promise and signature cues stable while varying situations. |

**For our ad, the strongest ingredient is the disproportion: extraordinary technology solving an ordinary annoyance.**

She has superhuman vision, speed, intelligence, and strength—and uses them to avoid being late. That gives us spectacle *and* personality. The calm “Morning” after an absurd rooftop chase is the payoff.

I’d strengthen the execution in five ways:

1. **Make the train unmistakably the same train.** Give it a distinctive yellow stripe or carriage number. Otherwise, viewers might interpret the ending as “she caught another train,” weakening the whole demonstration.

2. **Make the upgrade’s timeline clear.** Our current clinic cut could suggest she gets surgery while that train travels one stop. Either establish “the next morning,” or make the clinic sequence an unmistakable flashback. Confusion consumes attention we need for the joke.

3. **Make AFTERHUMAN responsible for the memorable moment.** Show its name naturally at the clinic and associate a distinctive activation sound with the implants. Repeat that sound with the final logo. A logo appearing only after the excitement risks being forgotten.

4. **Give the action contrast.** Fast cuts throughout can become monotonous. Use: slammed doors → brief stillness → upgrade clicks → explosive acceleration → sudden quiet → “Morning.” **The pause helps the speed feel faster and the joke land.**

5. **Choose one signature image.** For example, she runs alongside the moving train and makes eye contact with the passenger who watched her miss it. That’s a particularly clear visual expression of the promise.
